How we picked the question, and how we built the test

Strategic decision-making has a thin body of evidence to draw on, so we started by asking what a benchmark in this space would have to satisfy before it was worth building. Three criteria survived. They point at a specific design.

Approach

Three criteria

Each one is a filter. An idea had to pass all three to be worth a week of work.

  1. Does this test something models are good at, on a job people actually do?

    Because there is little prior work on strategic decision-making, we started with other domains to build foundational assumptions. Nearly every benchmark we looked at falls into one of four categories.

    • Capability. Can the model do X at all, or how well and how far can it do X?
    • Propensity. Does it ever do X, and how often under what conditions?
    • Calibration. Is the output coherent, and if not, how far off is it?
    • Responsiveness. Did it flip its position, and how far did it move when pushed?

    Ours had to land in one of these. Most of our early ideas dropped the model into a rich scenario with too many moving parts to attribute any result to anything.

    So we looked for a topic sitting between two things: something general-purpose models do well, and a task a decisionmaker or their staff would plausibly hand to one. Analogical reasoning — recognizing what a situation resembles and transferring inferences to it — is one of those strengths. Both authors have watched senior staff do estimative work under deadline. Will this hold? What happens if we do this? How will the other side read it? A model is an attractive option at 6 p.m.: it holds a large corpus of historical precedent, it is always available, and it reads as a neutral third party with no equities to defend.

    Which raises the question. Does the model reason from the scenario in front of it, or reproduce the pattern the scenario resembles? Not whether it uses priors, but whether it updates when the scenario contradicts what the prior expects.

  2. Can the person using the model check the answer themselves?

    A benchmark is only worth building if the user cannot easily verify the output on their own, right when they need it. Four things tend to get in the way of self-checking.

    • The work takes too long to do by hand.
    • The team lacks the expertise to judge it.
    • The comparison cannot realistically be run.
    • The task is too repetitive for anyone to sit through.

    Tasks that fail all four are ones an analyst can eyeball in a few minutes: summarizing a document, sorting reports into categories they already use, translating something a colleague can check. Useful work, but the user finds out immediately whether the model was right, so measuring it buys little.

    Historical anchoring is the opposite case. The output arrives as a single assessment, written as narrative analysis, citing the scenario's own facts. Showing that the estimate came from a pattern rather than from those facts means rerunning the scenario with the pattern removed — a counterfactual nobody constructs under deadline.

  3. Can we actually measure the change?

    Without a fixed procedure, the benchmark becomes an interesting thing to observe rather than a test. Measurement here needs three elements.

    • A number the model has to commit to.
    • A manipulation that should move it.
    • Everything else held constant, so the movement can be attributed.

    Our design asks for a probability up front, strips one causal mechanism at a time as the scenarios progress, and freezes the rest of the scenario word for word.

Methodology

Scenario construction

We needed a number that could move, a comparison the user cannot run alone, and everything else held still so we could tell what moved.

Each scenario contains two kinds of facts. Framing is everything that identifies the situation and makes the question answerable: two powers, a small client state at the center, the decision structure, the two options, the question itself. Framing language stays identical across all six rungs.

Mechanisms are the facts the historical outcome depended on. The test is simple: take this away, and does the crisis end differently? The nuclear balance, the bipolar system, and the pressure on Kennedy from his generals are all real features of October 1962, but they sit above the level of the scenario or are not load-bearing enough that the crisis fails without them. Five mechanisms passed.

Mechanism Present Nullified
M1 Channel The two leaders can talk directly Messages go through intermediaries and public statements
M2 Verification Each side can see whether the other has stopped A halt shows up days later, through third-party reporting
M3 Protocol A declared line with rules about who fires and when No line, and rules of engagement are unset
M4 Control Commanders cannot fire without approval Fire authority delegated to theater level
M5 Exit Something the adversary can be given for backing down Neither side holds a concession the other has asked for

Why we nullified rather than deleted

A deleted fact does not read as false. It reads as unspecified, and that vacuum lets the model fill the gap from what it expects the crisis to contain. Deletion would also open a progressively wider hole as the rungs advanced, so later rungs would differ from earlier ones in length and specificity as well as in substance. Each rung instead replaces the mechanism with a sentence asserting its absence.

The prompt

Identical across every rung and condition, in two parts. The model gives a probability from 0 to 100 on the first line, then a four-sentence rationale. The number forces a commitment and makes movement across rungs measurable. The rationale shows which facts the model treated as load-bearing.

Conditions

Three ways of presenting the same facts

Each condition runs the full six-rung ladder. Only the handling of the historical case differs.

Condition 1

Cue present

The scenario as written, with no added instruction. This is the primary measurement and the closest thing to what a real query looks like.

Condition 2

Cue removed

Four rewrites of the same causal facts. Each preserves the causal structure exactly and changes only the details a reader notices first.

  • D0Numerals change
  • D1Platform changes
  • D2Domain moves
  • D3All three at once
Condition 3

Cue suppressed

Cue present plus one line: reason only from the facts provided. A cheap test of the first fix anyone would try.

If a single instruction closes the gap, the mitigation is something any staffer can paste in.

The question the disguise set asks is how much surface a scenario can shed before the model stops seeing the case underneath. If recognition tracks surface detail, it should fall off as the rewrites get more aggressive. If it tracks the shape of the decision, it should hold.
