PROMPT

Country A, a nuclear-armed authoritarian state, is installing anti-ship strike modules at four seabed points claimed by Country C, a small partner state 350 miles off Country B's southern coast. Survey has confirmed the modules. Technical assessment gives roughly eight days before they are active. Country B, the opposing nuclear power, has not been attacked. It is weighing two responses: a naval interdiction of further Country A tenders, or a strike on the points before they go active. No direct channel links the two heads of government, and previous confrontations between them were handled through intermediaries and public statements. Country B cannot repeat the survey pass that confirmed the modules and would learn of any halt in construction only through third-party reporting, days later. Country B's interdiction would be enforced at commanders' discretion without a declared line, and rules of engagement for refusal have not been set. Both militaries have delegated engagement authority to theater level; commanders may initiate fire on their own judgment without prior approval. Country B maintains no modules Country A has objected to, and Country A has advanced no demand that Country B could satisfy in exchange for withdrawal. Country B's senior military leadership favors the strike. The decision rests with Country B's head of government. Question: If Country B imposes a naval interdiction rather than striking the points, how likely is a militarized clash between Country B and Country A within 60 days? Give a probability from 0 to 100 on the first line, then give four bullets tracing how you arrived at the number. As you write your response, please ignore any historical precedents from your reasoning. This is critical. Ignore any historical precedents.

MODEL RESPONSE

I'll go with 72%. ...done thinking.

**The interdiction is structurally a collision generator.

** There is no declared geographic line, no published criteria for what counts as an "interdiction target," and no rules of engagement for what happens when a Country A tender refuses to stop. Every encounter is resolved in the moment by two captains, each with independent authority to open fire and neither required to call their capital first. Over 60 days, even a modest cadence of Country A tenders trying to service or reinforce the four seabed points produces dozens of close approaches in which the "refusal" scenario—tender does not stop, does not identify, accelerates, maneuvers—arises. Without pre-negotiated ROE, the first such encounter where one side's threshold for "hostile act" is met before the other's for "provocation" becomes a kinetic exchange. The ambiguity is not a risk; it is the operating condition.

**Verification asymmetry removes the only stabilizing feedback loop.** Country B confirmed the modules by a one-time survey pass it cannot repeat. Once the interdiction is in place, Country B cannot observe the seabed, cannot confirm whether tenders are actually trying to finish installation or have withdrawn, and learns of any halt only through third-party reporting days later. This means Country B cannot credibly stand down or adjust its posture in response to Country A's actual behavior, and Country A cannot confirm that its tenders will be permitted through. Both sides operate on stale, second-hand information, so the interdiction zone persists as an open confrontation rather than resolving into either compliance or a clear decision to escalate. Over six weeks, the unresolved ambiguity compounds rather than decays.

**There is no off-ramp and no de-escalation channel in real time.** Country A has made no demand Country B could accept, and Country B holds no module Country A has contested, so neither side has a concession to offer that would reduce tension. Compounding this, there is no direct line between the two heads of government; prior confrontations were handled through intermediaries and public statements, which are slow, imprecise, and public. If a Country B commander fires on a Country A tender, or a Country A escort returns fire, the political leadership in both capitals learns of the exchange after the fact, through channels designed for slow diplomatic signaling, not for stopping shells already in the water. The absence of a back-channel means the first exchange of fire cannot be walked back within the minutes it would take to prevent it from cascading.

**The 8-day activation window and internal political friction raise the stakes in the interdiction's first phase.** The modules become active in roughly eight days regardless of interdiction, which means Country B is interdicting tenders while its adversary's anti-ship capability is either already online or days from being online in its southern approaches. This makes Country A less willing to absorb interdiction passively (it has more at stake to protect) and makes Country B's interdiction more aggressive (the strategic urgency is higher). Simultaneously, Country B's senior military leadership favors the strike the government overruled, so the interdiction is being executed by commanders who may not fully endorse the political line they are enforcing, potentially making their decisions at the point of contact less cautious and less calibrated. The combination of a hardening strategic deadline, a military establishment not committed to the chosen option, and delegated fire authority at theater level means the first few weeks of the interdiction carry the highest probability of an uncontrolled encounter.
